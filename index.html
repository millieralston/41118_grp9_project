Abstract

This project presents an autonomous robot pursuit system in which a Husky robot (the chaser) learns to track and intercept a second scripted Husky robot (the runner) within a bounded PyBullet physics simulation. The system integrates two complementary approaches: reinforcement learning for high-level pursuit behaviour, and computer vision for real-time target detection.

The chaser is trained using Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) via Stable Baselines3, with an MLP policy operating on a compact 8-dimensional observation vector. This vector encodes the runner's position and the three nearest obstacles in the chaser's local frame, computed by a dedicated perception module. A shaped reward function incentivises closing distance to the runner, penalises obstacle and wall collisions, and awards a large terminal bonus of 800 upon successful capture. A reactive obstacle avoidance reflex supplements the learned policy by blending short-range collision responses directly into the chaser's wheel commands, ensuring the agent can recover from tight situations that the PPO policy alone may not handle.

In parallel, a YOLOv8 object detection model was trained on frames captured from the chaser's onboard simulated camera, enabling the system to visually identify the runner robot in real time. During demonstration, the trained PPO agent operates the chaser in a live PyBullet GUI session while the YOLO stream annotates detected targets on the camera feed.

The environment randomises obstacle placement, starting positions, and runner heading each episode, promoting generalisation. Results demonstrate that the chaser successfully intercepts the actively-evading runner across diverse configurations, validating the effectiveness of combining learned pursuit policies with vision-based perception in a simulated autonomous robotics context.

Reinforcement Learning

The chaser is trained using Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) via Stable Baselines3, with an MLP policy operating on a compact 8-dimensional observation vector. This vector encodes the runner's position and the three nearest obstacles in the chaser's local frame, computed by a dedicated perception module. A shaped reward function incentivises closing distance to the runner, penalises obstacle and wall collisions, and awards a large terminal bonus of 800 upon successful capture. A reactive obstacle avoidance reflex supplements the learned policy by blending short-range collision responses directly into the chaser's wheel commands, ensuring the agent can recover from tight situations that the PPO policy alone may not handle.

MLP policy architecture and hyperparameters were selected based on preliminary experiments, with a network of 2 hidden layers and 64 neurons per layer providing a good balance of learning capacity and training stability. The reward function was carefully shaped to encourage pursuit behaviour while discouraging unsafe actions, with the terminal capture bonus significantly outweighing intermediate rewards to prioritise successful interception. The obstacle avoidance reflex was implemented as a simple proportional controller that generates wheel commands to steer away from detected obstacles within a short range, and was blended with the PPO policy outputs using a weighted sum to allow the learned policy to focus on pursuit strategy while relying on the reflex for immediate collision avoidance. Training was conducted over 100,000 timesteps, with the agent achieving a capture success rate of over 90% in evaluation episodes after convergence.

YOLO/CNN

The visual detection component uses YOLOv8, a convolutional neural network architecture, trained entirely on synthetic data generated from within the PyBullet simulation. A dedicated dataset collection script rendered 3,500 frames from the chaser robot's simulated onboard camera, a 60° FOV perspective at 640×480 resolution across randomised episode resets, capturing the runner robot at a wide distribution of distances (1–16 metres) and lateral offsets. Ground-truth bounding boxes were derived automatically from PyBullet's pixel-level segmentation mask rather than manual annotation: each frame's segmentation buffer was queried for pixels belonging to the runner's body ID, and the resulting mask was converted directly to YOLO-format normalised bounding box coordinates (class, x_center, y_center, width, height). A two-stage quality filter rejected ambiguous samples, discarding any detection with fewer than 200 visible runner pixels or a visibility ratio below 0.5 (i.e. where occlusion made the bounding box unreliable), before labels were written to disk. The dataset was split into training and validation sets and fine-tuned over 50 epochs with early stopping (patience of 20), building on a previously trained checkpoint to progressively improve detection accuracy. At inference time during the demo, the trained model runs on live camera frames from the simulation and annotates detected runner instances in real time, demonstrating a full sim-to-model visual perception pipeline.

This section presents the quantitative and qualitative outcomes of both the reinforcement learning (PPO) component and the computer vision (YOLO) component of the TipBot system, followed by an assessment of their combined integration.

Because each episode randomises start positions, obstacle layouts, and runner motion, TensorBoard metrics are noisy and should be treated as indicative trends rather than a single success criterion. We therefore pair the TensorBoard data with qualitative demo footage and secondary metrics such as ep_len_mean, collision counts, capture rate, and distance-to-runner behaviour.

Discussion

PPO Policy Effectiveness

The PPO-based pursuit policy demonstrated robust target interception capability within the randomised PyBullet environment, achieving a 71% success rate and consistent reward growth across training. The policy's ability to navigate a dynamic arena with randomised obstacle placement, start positions, and runner headings — without ever falling over or getting permanently stuck — validates the core reinforcement learning approach.

The reward shaping design proved effective overall. The progress-based reward (scaled by ×60) provided a strong primary learning signal, while the close-range bonus (1.8× within 5.0 units) and capture bonus (+800 within 2.3 units) established a clear reward gradient guiding the agent through approach and interception phases.

Key Limitation

The policy's reliance on supplementary heuristics — the obstacle avoidance shield and close-range assist — constrains how much emergent pursuit behaviour PPO can develop autonomously. The close-range assist in particular may suppress the development of more sophisticated interception strategies the policy could otherwise learn.

YOLO Detection Quality & Its Limitations for Control

Despite achieving near-perfect validation accuracy, the YOLOv11n model proved insufficient for direct integration into the PPO observation space. This result — while initially surprising given the metrics — is consistent with known challenges in perception-to-control transfer. Several factors likely contributed:

  • Observation noise sensitivity: PPO's MLP policy was trained on clean ground-truth coordinates. Even small YOLO-derived positional errors introduce distributional shift the policy is not robust to, causing reward collapse.
  • Temporal inconsistency: YOLO operates frame-by-frame without temporal smoothing. The resulting jitter in the normalised position estimate produces non-stationary noise that destabilises PPO's value function.
  • Occlusion events: Missed detections behind obstacles cause the position signal to temporarily disappear. Without a fallback, the policy receives a zero or stale observation outside its training distribution.
  • Domain gap within simulation: Camera angle, lighting, and obstacle configurations during live PPO rollouts may differ subtly from the training distribution, amplifying error rates beyond validation metrics.

Integration Architecture: Lessons Learned

The core challenge exposed by this project is the mismatch between the requirements of a high-performing visual detector and those of a stable RL observation source. A detector optimised for accuracy under static evaluation conditions does not automatically satisfy the temporal consistency, completeness, and low-variance requirements of a policy's observation stream.

Future Architecture Recommendations

Apply temporal tracking (Kalman filtering or SORT) to smooth YOLO detections and provide continuity during occlusion. Train PPO from scratch with YOLO observations rather than substituting into a pre-trained policy. Augment the YOLO training dataset with obstacle classes to reduce occlusion-related false negatives.

The project highlights a broader design lesson: in hybrid RL–vision systems, the interface between perception and control is often the most fragile component. The quality of the learned detector is a necessary but insufficient condition — the representation and reliability of the signal passed to the policy matters as much as raw detection accuracy.

System-Level Assessment

Taken together, TipBot demonstrates a successful proof-of-concept for RL-based target interception in a simulated environment, with a well-trained visual perception module that is functionally ready for further integration work. The 71% success rate across randomised episodes is a strong baseline, particularly given the relatively short training budget of 120,000 timesteps and the complexity of the obstacle-filled arena.

The failure to achieve stable vision-in-the-loop control is a meaningful negative result that clarifies the next engineering steps rather than undermining the system's foundations. Scaling training to 500,000 timesteps with n_steps=1024, combined with object tracking and end-to-end vision-based policy training, represents a credible roadmap toward a fully autonomous perception-driven interception agent.

Conclusion

TipBot successfully demonstrated ground-based target interception through a combination of Proximal Policy Optimisation and YOLOv11n computer vision in a PyBullet simulation environment. The PPO agent achieved a 71% interception success rate across randomised episodes with zero stuck or fall-over failures, confirming the viability of the RL-based pursuit approach. The YOLO detector achieved near-perfect validation accuracy (mAP50 > 0.99), establishing a high-quality visual perception pipeline.

The primary unresolved challenge is the stable integration of vision-based observations into the policy's control loop. The instability observed in CNN–RL joint training underscores that detection quality and control-compatible signal reliability are distinct requirements that must both be satisfied. Addressing this through object tracking, increased training scale, and end-to-end vision-policy co-training represents the clearest path to a fully autonomous, perception-driven interception system.

Next Steps

Scale PPO training to 500,000 timesteps (n_steps=1024)  ·  Apply Kalman/SORT tracking to YOLO outputs  ·  Train end-to-end with vision observations  ·  Add obstacle classes to YOLO dataset  ·  Reduce reliance on hardcoded heuristics

Reflection

This project was a rewarding exploration of integrating reinforcement learning and computer vision for autonomous robotic pursuit. The PPO agent successfully learned to chase and intercept the runner across diverse scenarios, while the YOLO-based perception pipeline provided real-time visual detection capabilities. We encountered many challenges with tuning our logic to result in a successful policy and a robust perception model, but the final system demonstrates the potential of combining learned control policies with vision-based perception in a simulated robotics context.

As the person who worked on our environment settup, GUI and portfolio, I found this project to be a great opportunity to apply my knowledge of Python and PyBullet in a practical context. The main challenge I had in participating in this as a group project was that because my fellow team members were leading the charge on MLP and CNN development it was hard for me to find space to meaningfully contribute. And as the portfolio was inexplicably dependent on the completion of their work, it was hard for me to contribute effectively until the last stage of integration. The way we chose to integrate the policies with each other meant that our final results were not seen to quite last minute, and I would have liked to have had more time to work on the GUI and portfolio, but I am proud of the work we did and the final result we achieved.

I am not sure how we could have better collaborated on this coding project, perhaps using more of a back and forth approach in pushing code to the repository and integrating it as we went, rather than waiting for the final stages to integrate everything together, and for other group members to see code worked on locally sooner. I think this would have allowed us to see the results of our work sooner and iterate on it more effectively.
